# FORGE — shared test-station disruption recovery

**Focused PRD v0.3 · 26 September 2026**

FORGE is an AI recovery agent for shared test-station failures in liquid-cooling manufacturing. The hackathon proves one workflow in one plant. This document supersedes the broad platform pitch; the original PRD is preserved in `docs/PRD-platform-v0.2.md` as background.

## The customer problem

When a shared leak, flow, or thermal test station goes down, near-finished units queue up, product lines compete for the remaining qualified capacity, and shipment commitments become uncertain. Production, quality, maintenance, and logistics need one concrete answer: which orders should we test next, on which qualified station, at what cost, and who owns the recovery?

**Buyer:** VP Manufacturing, Plant Manager, or VP Operations at a liquid-cooling manufacturer. **Decision maker during an incident:** plant manager. **Execution owner:** test supervisor, coordinating quality and shipping.

## The wedge: only five capabilities in the pitch

1. Detect the orders connected to the failed test station.
2. Calculate the customer commitments that will miss without recovery.
3. Identify remaining qualified and calibrated test capacity.
4. Generate and compare two or three feasible recovery plans.
5. Route the selected plan to the plant manager, then give the supervisor exact execution tasks.

One physical disruption must lead to a concrete, reviewable recovery decision. A generalized operations dashboard, multi-company tour, supply-chain platform, or open-ended enterprise chatbot is not the pitch.

## Golden demonstration

At 08:00, **T-02** reports a thermal-controller fault and an **8-hour expected outage**. The synthetic queue contains **14 orders**, including **three shipment commitments due within 24 hours**. Without recovery, those three commitments miss and **$1.4 million of shipment value is at risk**. T-01 has only **6 regular hours** available within the next 24 hours.

These figures are calculated from `fixtures/test-outage.json`, not hard-coded UI claims and not claimed to reproduce a real manufacturer's operations. The existing CoolIT enterprise pack supplies background context; it does not contain the station-level inputs or commercial amounts required for this incident. The new REC order identities are deliberately separate from that pack's COM commitments and known inconsistent allocation links.

| Recovery | Action | Default result | Main trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Prioritize the highest-priority shipment using regular qualified capacity | Protect $900,000; two lower-priority commitments remain late | No overtime, but $500,000 remains exposed |
| B | Add up to six hours of qualified-team overtime on T-01 | Protect all three urgent commitments | $7,800 overtime; $11,800 total estimated incident cost |
| C | Reassign qualified loop/manifold work to validation cell T-03 within the same plant | Protect all three urgent commitments | $8,900 setup/staffing; $12,900 total estimated incident cost |

**Single-site decision:** Plan C is an internal qualified alternative, as explicitly selected by the user. No inter-site transfer, additional factory, or qualification assumption outside this plant is included. T-04 is visible but excluded due to expired calibration. T-03 cannot test CDUs.

Changing the outage duration recalculates risk, available windows, schedules, costs, and the recommendation. The selected recovery and all approval/task states are bound to the exact run and input fingerprint.

## What “feasible” means

The deterministic service models:

- one whole-order, non-preemptive test at a time per station;
- released and test-ready orders;
- station/product-family qualification and valid calibration;
- regular shifts, explicit overtime, alternate-cell readiness, and outage exclusion;
- whole-order test durations that include setup;
- a two-hour post-test release/packing buffer;
- a 96-hour planning horizon.

A feasible schedule can still miss a shipment deadline. That is a visible trade-off, not an application error. An unscheduled job or a hard-gate violation blocks plan selection. The no-recovery baseline keeps the original T-02 queue; recovery plans reassign only to eligible resources. Schedules are generated by a deterministic priority/earliest-finish heuristic, not a claim of global optimization. Actual test passes, component availability, transport, production labor rosters, and customer acceptance are conditional assumptions, not certified outcomes.

The default recommendation minimizes remaining shipment value at risk, then estimated incremental incident cost, among feasible plans. Objective weights are intentionally fixed and inspectable for the hackathon.

## Economic calculation

Separate **exposure** from **cost**. Shipment value at risk is the sum of shipment values for no-recovery late commitments; it is not automatically lost revenue and must never be added to cost estimates.

Estimated incremental incident cost is the sum of:

| Component | Synthetic formula |
| --- | --- |
| Overtime | Actual modeled T-01 overtime hours × $1,300 qualified-team hourly rate |
| Premium freight allowance | Remaining late orders × $1,250; this allowance does not change the forecast shipment date |
| Idle labor | Outage hours × 6 operators × $65/hour |
| Idle equipment | Outage hours × $110/hour |
| Added WIP holding | Extra unit-hours vs the no-outage schedule × $2.50/unit-hour |
| Potential late-delivery penalties | Remaining late shipment value × 2%; conditional allowance, not an asserted contractual penalty |
| Alternate-cell reassignment | 2-hour changeover × $850/hour + 6-hour staffing reservation × $1,200/hour when T-03 is used |

Show WIP accumulation separately in unit-hours. Show customer escalation risk as the number of late priority-1/2 commitments; do not invent a monetary score. Failed-station idle exposure remains in every recovery plan. All rates, durations, shipment values, and calendars must be inspectable in the UI.

## Approval and handoff

The hackathon state machine is:

`AWAITING_SELECTION → AWAITING_MANAGER → AWAITING_SUPERVISOR → EXECUTING_SIMULATION → SIMULATION_COMPLETE`

1. Route a feasible plan to the plant manager.
2. Record the manager's name, rationale, selected plan, time, and immutable input fingerprint.
3. Generate station-specific test tasks, including order, units, assigned station, planned start/end, and responsible role.
4. Record the supervisor's name and acceptance.
5. Permit simulated task completion only after prerequisite steps. Readiness verification precedes test tasks; tests on the same station follow their planned sequence. Each order has its own passing-test verification, release, packing, and shipping task after its test, so urgent shipments do not wait for the rest of the queue.
6. Persist events and completion receipts locally. Duplicate approval/acceptance/task completion must not produce duplicate effects.
7. Recalculation supersedes the previous run. Stale run IDs cannot be approved or executed.

This is an explicit local role simulation. Entered names are not authenticated identities, there is no external notification, and no operation is written to ERP/MES, equipment, logistics, or a customer. Completing simulated tasks is not evidence of a passing test or actual shipment. Never report operational recovery as observed without outcome evidence.

## AI role

Anthropic explains the current deterministic incident calculation, trade-offs, qualification exclusions, and supervisor tasks. It can retrieve the original enterprise pack for additional context, but cannot use historical COM records to overwrite current REC scenario facts. The model does not calculate the authoritative plan, approve a decision, release a lot, or complete a task.

Responses cite the current calculation, fixture assumptions, or original source records. Without an API key, the UI shows the real calculated incident brief and retrieval results, clearly labeled. It does not simulate an AI answer. Keys remain server-side.

## UI

Preserve the three-column working layout and supplied visual identity:

- **Left:** affected orders, shipment value, deadline, and no-recovery risk; filter to urgent or late orders.
- **Middle:** outage control, immediate impact, shared test-capacity diagram, selected-order consequences, three plans, economic breakdown, approval and supervisor tasks.
- **Right:** incident-specific recovery agent with cited explanations.

The primary page has one plant and one incident. Company switching and the broad enterprise-data workspace are removed from the pitch surface; the existing workspace remains available at `/workspace` for development/reference use.

## Proof and acceptance

- The default eight-hour outage produces 14 affected orders and three missed commitments totaling $1.4M.
- Plan A protects $900K and exposes the two remaining late commitments. Plan B protects all urgent shipments with exactly six overtime hours/$7,800 overtime. Plan C uses only qualified T-03 work inside the same plant.
- Every order is conserved once per schedule; no resource overlap, outage overlap, invalid qualification/calibration, partial test, or unscheduled selected plan is allowed.
- Identical fixture/version/outage inputs produce identical schedules and fingerprint.
- Outage changes recalculate all derived impact and cost fields.
- Exposure is not double-counted as cost.
- Approval and supervisor acceptance are separate persisted actions; stale-run and task-prerequisite checks run server-side.
- Current incident context and citations reach Anthropic without exposing the API key.
- Measured calculation latency is displayed separately from the human decision cycle.

**Demo target:** find a viable recovery in under 60 seconds from the injected failure. This is a target and a timing experiment, not a validated comparison with real human teams. Record time to computed plans, time to manager decision, and time to supervisor acceptance separately. A customer pilot must establish the manual coordination baseline and validate actual shipment outcomes before any speed or savings claim.

## Pitch

Liquid-cooling manufacturers must complete required tests before releasing shipments. When a shared test station fails, multiple lines compete for the remaining capacity and customer promises are suddenly at risk. FORGE identifies the affected orders, calculates which commitments will miss, compares qualified recovery plans, and turns the plant manager's decision into exact supervisor tasks.

**Demo close:** “One test station fails. FORGE shows the commitments at risk and a feasible recovery plan before the coordination scramble takes over.”

Later, the same evidence-and-approval loop may extend to other factory constraints and learn from measured outcomes. That vision stays in the background. The hackathon proves test-station disruption recovery.
